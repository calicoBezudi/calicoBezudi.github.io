Case Study

Creating Camera Occlusion for an Isometric Game.

Many people use "Isometric" loosely in place of Top Down when describing cameras. The truth is, however, that actual orthographic projection comes with a unique set of traits and challenges.

Unreal engine is an incredibly powerful piece of software. It handles many complex interactions and design struggles such as inputs, character and controller sytem relationships, materials and post processing in an intuitive, powerful, and refined way. There are, however, parts of unreal which are not fully developed for every usecase, and it is inevitable throughout the course of your development that you may eventually run into these.
In our case, the Orthographic camera projection system inside of Unreal works wonderfully and out of the box in theory, but faces unique chalenges in application. A great example of this is in Occlusion. Occlusion in unreal relies on understanding the position of the camera aswell as the scene depth of objects and the position of the player in screenspace. Many of these parts are imperfect or nonfunctional when using orthographic projection; So, when we are designing a game that relies on the player moving throughout indoor spaces where walls may need to occlude, we need to come up with unique solutions for these challenges.
Moving between rooms, rotating the camera to solve puzzles, and exploring the environment are all aspects of Garden of Idan in its original design. Ontop of this, we wanted to include isometric illusion puzzles which would require some planning and forethought in things such as the pitch of the isometric camera in designing the spaces.
Being an isometric puzzle game was part of Garden of Idans original design and pitch from the start. The camera system would be its key player, and puzzles would be built around it so nailing it very adamantly and from the start would be important. Keeping it modular and easy to use would also be key, and so we have to start with defining all they key traits of our camera. Sketches like the one above are important in defining things that people may take for granted or believe everyone is on the same page on, but may actually be miscommunicating or leaving undefined. Whether or not the camera is centered on the player or the room being an important one, what the space around a room looks like, and so on.
Not Orthographic
Orthographic (-45°)
One of my personal gripes has always been when someone describes a game as "Isometric," when it infact is only top-down. There are many top offenders for this, such as League of Legends or Baldurs Gate 3 (A series who, historically, has actually had Isometric titles such as the original Baldurs Gate.) These games do not use Orthographic projection, and are merely top-down, and so the use of "Isometric" is not very applicable beyond calling to a lineage of games of that nature. 

To visualize orthographic projection, you can look at the above example at two examples of a camera angled in the same way (at -45°), in the same spot. One is using a perspective camera at 60° FOV, and the other uses an Orthographic camera (which, actually, does not have a Field of View but rather orthographic width in the engine, which defines how much space is within the view of the screen.

In the above examples, you can see the difference quite clearly; particularly in how space is warped with the perspective lens stretching and bending the space at the edges to be larger, and shrinking the floor tiles in the far corner, whilst the Orthographic projection keeps everything Uniform.


Orthographic projections do not capture depth. This is incredibly key, and is what allows the magic of Isometric games like Monument Valley to work.


So if games that are often labeled "Isometric" arent really Isometric, what does isometric mean? Many agreeably Isometric games which use Orthographic projection, infact even, are not all "True Isometric". A great example of this is the game Project Zomboid, which does use Orthographic projection, but is actually Dimetric, and not Isometric.


θ = tan -1 ( b a 2 )
This formula is used to find the camera pitch needed for orthographic projection that aligns parts of world space along a 45° yaw rotation. For every a units moved along each axis in horizontal space (X,Y), you move up b spaces vertically to overlap two points within the orthographic view. For example...
θ = tan -1 ( 400 400 2 ) θ = tan -1 ( 1 1 2 )
Garden of Idan used 200x200 and 400x400 as its basis for floor tiles in the game. The above function returns the angle used for True Isometric projection. 

If I had a floor tile at (0,0,0), and a floor tile at (100,100,100), or (400,400,400), they would all three overlap perfectly in True Isometric Projection along a 45° yaw rotation. This is considered True Isometric Projection, at -35.264°. To use this, you would create an Orthographic camera with its rotation set to  (0,-35.264, 45)

θ = tan -1 ( 300 400 2 ) θ = tan -1 ( 3 4 2 )

However, when using this illusion in our games, we found that it was slightly too tall and made our rooms strange heights and sizes ingame with very tall staircases. You would need to move up many stories just to have one floor line up with another, without creating too many unwanted illusion effects!

Our solution was to forego a true isometric projection to create our own Orthographic view. Too steep of an angle would make it too difficult to design a room for illusions, and too flat of an angle would create too many unwanted illusions. With testing, we opted for 300 units up for every 400 units traveled on each horizontal axis, or a pitch of -27.938° and this view worked perfectly for the height of rooms we wanted within the game. And while this isnt True Isometric, it is common for "Isometric" games to use orthographic projections which best suit their gameplay such as Dimetric for a 2:1 pixel ratio, and still be called generally "Isometric" as a catchall term.

Defining this angle is important to do early on and to commit to, so rigorous testing and planning is demanded. To test it, we made our first room in the game apply this illusion. We built our assets around this principle, with stairs going up 300 units, and our walls and floor tiles having 400x400 and 200x200 segments to create common denominators and a wider range of heights to plan around. Constructed with the other room, and greyboxed with the other scene parts, we begin to see other challenges arise that we will need to face later. The main dividing wall demands that it will need to be occluded, and that single sided meshes will not suffice for our finished setup.
This is an overview and high level view of how the main parts of our camera system is constructed behind the scenes. The camera is detached from the player entirely, and the two systems communicate using interfaces and blueprint function libraries. Our player pawn and the IsoCam container are both created at runtime on BeginPlay. The player controls the rotation of the camera. 

When the player enters a Camera Zone, the Camera Zone activates an anchor point to move the camera container to. The Camer container handles a lot of the inner logic used for occlusion, updating actors in the scene which need to respond to the camera's movement, and holds a spring arm with a child actor containing the actual camera itself to smooth its movement.  

Here, you can see the usage of the blueprint function library to activate and resolve parts of the system, as well as to move the camera container and pass along some values for the Occlusion system I'll go into more detail on later.
This is an overview of the player controller's handling of the camera rotation. I make powerful use of the Gamplay Camera System introduced in the latest versions of unreal. I gained familiarity with this system during my time developing Cold Fusion in 5.5, and with updates in 5.6 I use camera rotation dampening nodes to create a smooth way to handle the rotation of the camera that was easy to iterate, design, and test.
You can see here a demonstration of the camera system during development, with functional camera rotation, and moving between anchors to activate rooms.
And here, an example of how systems could easily be attached to the anchors of a specific room in order to respond to the actions of the camera rotating within its space. This is used frequently throughout the game, for many major puzzles and for activating the illusion mechanic that allows the player to walk up to the top floor. with the broken staircase.
And now, we get into occlusion. Without having a way to find what exists between the player and the camera, the limited usage of scene depth and other struggles with the Orthographic projection, I had to find a solution to create the Occlusion effect we desired from my original design illustration. Upon reflection, even if I had position the camera itself in a "faux" position in space to try and get the occlusion material nodes in the gameplay camera system to function, they still would not give me the full two wall occlusion we were hoping to get.

This video here shows my first attempt at solving occlusion by attaching a very large collision box to the camera container and seeing if it can iterate through the actors it hits to hide or adjust their materials. This, of course, was not very performant, especially on a camera that rotates and updates frequently. I also tried other features such as raycasting, but it was not very accurate, jittered, and struggled to hit all the actors I wanted. 

Material parameter collections, aswell as math became our solution. If we had the location of the camera container in world space passed as a Vector3 , we could use the relationship of the pixel passed through the material graph to that position in worldspace to create a dithered mask for occlusion.

This is a demonstration of the material displaying a reactivity to the world space location of the camera. This can be represented as a cube with Green, Red, Yellow, Blue, Magenta, Cyan, White and Black corners. This reveals the underlying Vector 3 value: XYZ 1,1,1 being all white, 0,0,0 being all black, 1,0,0 being all red, and so on. This might not be the most intuitive thing, but a great way to understand this is that Z inXYZ, the third value in the Vector three representing the vertical axis, is tantamount to B, the third value in the Vector three representing the color blue. All of the squares above the camera container have a positive Z, or in this case, incorperate blue.

This is a great way to remember which color arrow in unreal is which axis. Red is X, Green is Y, and Blue iz Z (RGB -> XYZ).
